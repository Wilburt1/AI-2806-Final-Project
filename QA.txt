## Data Attributes
Q: What is the definition of data dimensionality?
A: Data dimensionality refers to the number of features in a dataset. Low dimensionality means a small number of features, while high dimensionality means a large number of features.

Q: What are the challenges associated with high-dimensional data?
A: Challenges include: 1) High data sampling requirements (e.g., 10^11 vs. 10^3), 2) Increased computational complexity, 3) Overfitting, reduced robustness, and accuracy issues, 4) Difficulty in visualization, and 5) Challenges in explanation.

Q: What is the curse of dimensionality?
A: The curse of dimensionality refers to the difficulties in working with data in high-dimensional spaces, such as images, videos, or large text datasets, due to increased complexity and data requirements.

Q: What is data imbalance, and what are its consequences?
A: Data imbalance occurs when the distribution of classes in a dataset is not uniform, with one class significantly outnumbering others. It can lead to biased models that perform well on the majority class but poorly on the minority class.

Q: What is the bias-variance tradeoff in machine learning?
A: The bias-variance tradeoff is the balance between a model's simplicity (high bias, underfitting) and complexity (high variance, overfitting). High bias models are too simple and miss data complexity, while high variance models overfit and fail to generalize.

Q: What is data heterogeneity?
A: Data heterogeneity refers to collecting data from diverse sources, including syntactic heterogeneity (diverse data types, formats, encodings) and semantic heterogeneity (diverse semantic categories, e.g., 1000 classes in ImageNet).

Q: What is data volume, and how has it evolved over time?
A: Data volume refers to the size, amount, and scale of data. Datasets have grown by 0.1 to 0.2 orders of magnitude per year, translating to ~7 orders of magnitude over ~30 years, from 1e3 to 1e12 samples in AI training datasets.

Q: What is feature engineering in the context of data attributes?
A: Feature engineering involves constructing components (features) of each input sample to improve model performance, addressing challenges posed by high-dimensional data.

Q: What are some examples of data imbalance in real-world applications?
A: Examples include medical diagnosis (healthy vs. non-healthy populations), fraud detection (0.1% fraudulent vs. 99.9% legitimate transactions), and image recognition (85% cat images vs. 15% dog images).

Q: How can the bias-variance tradeoff be managed?
A: Techniques like cross-validation and regularization help manage the bias-variance tradeoff by balancing model complexity and generalization.

Q: What is overfitting, and how does it relate to high-dimensional data?
A: Overfitting occurs when a model is too complex, capturing noise in training data and failing to generalize. High-dimensional data increases overfitting risk due to sparse data and complex models.

Q: What is the impact of data volume on processing performance?
A: High data volume creates processing bottlenecks, addressed by techniques like parallel processing, data partitioning, and advanced hardware (CPUs, GPUs, TPUs).

Q: How does data sampling requirement differ between low and high-dimensional data?
A: Low-dimensional data requires fewer samples (e.g., 10^3), while high-dimensional data needs significantly more (e.g., 10^11) to achieve generalization.

Q: What is syntactic heterogeneity in data?
A: Syntactic heterogeneity refers to diversity in data types, formats, and encodings, such as multimodal data in audio-visual applications.

Q: What is an example of semantic heterogeneity?
A: Semantic heterogeneity includes diversity in semantic categories, such as 1000 classes in the ImageNet dataset for image classification.

## Data Structures
Q: What is a data structure?
A: A data structure is a way of storing and organizing data in a computer to enable efficient use, tailored to specific applications.

Q: What are the categories of data structures?
A: Categories include: 1) Primitive (e.g., integer, float, string, Boolean), 2) Linear (e.g., array, list, linked list, queue, stack), and 3) Non-linear (e.g., tree, binary tree, graph, hash table).

Q: How do arrays, graphs, and trees differ?
A: Arrays are linear, storing elements contiguously. Graphs are non-linear with nodes connected by edges, allowing cycles. Trees are hierarchical, non-linear, with each node having one parent and no cycles.

Q: What is the time complexity for accessing an element in an array?
A: Accessing an element in an array by index has a time complexity of O(1).

Q: What is a linked list, and what is it good for?
A: A linked list is a linear data structure where elements are stored in nodes linked by pointers, ideal for insertion and deletion operations.

Q: What is the difference between a queue and a stack?
A: A queue uses a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) strategy, while a stack uses a Last-In-First-Out (LIFO) strategy.

Q: What are some applications of graph data structures?
A: Graphs are used in solving complex problems like finding shortest paths (e.g., Dijkstra’s algorithm), network analysis, and social network modeling.

Q: Why are binary trees essential in data management?
A: Binary trees are versatile for efficient data management, retrieval, and processing due to their hierarchical structure with at most two children per node.

Q: What is a hash table, and how does it work?
A: A hash table stores data using keys as indices, computed by a hash function, enabling fast retrieval with average-case O(1) time complexity.

Q: What is binary search, and when is it used with arrays?
A: Binary search is an O(log n) algorithm for finding an element in a sorted array by repeatedly dividing the search interval in half.

Q: What is a directed graph, and how does it differ from an undirected graph?
A: A directed graph has ordered edge pairs (edges have direction), while an undirected graph has unordered pairs, where (v,w) implies (w,v).

Q: What is a weighted graph?
A: A weighted graph assigns weights to edges, representing costs like distance or time, used in algorithms like Dijkstra’s.

Q: What is the role of a list in data structures?
A: A list is a linear sequence of elements from various data types, supporting dynamic operations like appending or removing elements.

## Decision Trees
Q: What is a decision tree?
A: A decision tree is a tree structure where each node represents a feature, each branch represents an attribute value, and each leaf represents an outcome. It mimics rule-based human reasoning for tasks like classification and regression.

Q: What is the ID3 algorithm used for in decision trees?
A: The ID3 algorithm builds a decision tree top-down by selecting features at each node based on entropy and information gain as splitting criteria.

Q: How is a terminal node detected in a decision tree?
A: A terminal node is detected when all instances at the node belong to the same class, no further features are available, or a predefined depth or purity threshold is reached.

Q: What is an example of a decision tree application?
A: Predicting whether golf will be played based on weather features (outlook, temperature, humidity, wind) using a dataset of 14 days.

Q: How does a decision tree store its structure?
A: A decision tree is stored as a lookup table, mapping features, attribute values, and outcomes for efficient decision-making.

Q: What is the splitting criterion in the ID3 algorithm?
A: The ID3 algorithm uses information gain, calculated as the reduction in entropy, to decide which feature to assign to a node.

Q: How does a decision tree handle continuous attributes?
A: Continuous attributes are discretized by selecting thresholds that maximize information gain, creating binary or multi-way splits.

Q: What is the advantage of decision trees in classification tasks?
A: Decision trees are interpretable, mimicking human reasoning, and can handle both categorical and numerical data effectively.

Q: What is a leaf node in a decision tree?
A: A leaf node represents the final outcome (e.g., class label or continuous value) after traversing the tree based on feature conditions.

## Entropy and Information Gain
Q: What is entropy in the context of decision trees?
A: Entropy measures the uncertainty or average information bits in a dataset, calculated as E(S) = -∑(Pr(c) * log2(Pr(c))) for classes c in set S.

Q: What is information gain?
A: Information gain (IG(S,A)) is the reduction in entropy after splitting a dataset S based on feature A, calculated as IG(S,A) = E(S) - E(S,A).

Q: How is entropy used in the ID3 algorithm?
A: In the ID3 algorithm, entropy is used to evaluate the uncertainty of a dataset, and features with the highest information gain (lowest entropy after splitting) are chosen for nodes.

Q: What is an example of calculating information gain?
A: For a weather dataset, the information gain for "Outlook" is 0.247 (E(S) = 0.940, E(S,Outlook) = 0.693), higher than for "Temperature" (0.029), making it the first split.

Q: What does zero entropy indicate in a dataset?
A: Zero entropy indicates complete certainty, where all instances belong to a single class, requiring no further splitting.

Q: How is entropy applied in real-world scenarios?
A: Entropy is used in information theory for tasks like data compression (e.g., JPEG) and in machine learning to build decision trees for classification.

Q: What is the entropy of an attribute in a dataset?
A: The entropy of an attribute E(S,A) is the weighted average entropy of subsets created by splitting dataset S on attribute A.

Q: Why is information gain preferred over raw entropy in decision trees?
A: Information gain measures the reduction in uncertainty, directly indicating how useful a feature is for splitting, unlike raw entropy, which only measures uncertainty.

## Greedy Algorithms
Q: What is a greedy algorithm?
A: A greedy algorithm makes locally optimal choices at each step, hoping to achieve a globally optimal solution. It is simpler to code but may not always yield optimal results.

Q: What is Dijkstra’s algorithm, and what problem does it solve?
A: Dijkstra’s algorithm is a greedy algorithm that solves the single-source shortest path problem in a graph with non-negative edge weights, finding the shortest path from a starting node to all other nodes.

Q: How does Dijkstra’s algorithm work?
A: It initializes distances, picks a starting node, iteratively computes distances to unvisited nodes, updates shortest paths, and marks nodes as visited until all nodes are processed.

Q: What is an example of a real-world application of Dijkstra’s algorithm?
A: Finding the shortest route between airports, where edge weights represent distance, cost, or time.

Q: What is the difference between breadth-first search and depth-first search in graph traversal?
A: Breadth-first search explores nodes level by level using a queue (FIFO), while depth-first search explores as far as possible along a path using a stack (LIFO).

Q: Why might a greedy algorithm fail to find an optimal solution?
A: Greedy algorithms may fail because local optimal choices do not always lead to a global optimum, depending on the problem structure.

Q: How is a graph stored as an adjacency matrix?
A: An adjacency matrix is a square matrix where entry (i,j) is 1 if there is an edge from node i to node j, and 0 otherwise, suitable for dense graphs.

Q: What is an adjacency list, and when is it preferred?
A: An adjacency list stores, for each node, a list of its neighbors, preferred for sparse graphs due to lower memory usage compared to an adjacency matrix.

Q: What is the shortest path problem in weighted graphs?
A: The shortest path problem finds the path with the minimum total edge weight (e.g., miles) between two vertices in a weighted graph.

Q: What is a self-loop in a graph?
A: A self-loop is an edge (v,v) connecting a node to itself, typically assumed absent in most graph algorithms.

## Proximity Measures
Q: What is the difference between similarity and dissimilarity in proximity measures?
A: Similarity measures how alike two data points are (higher values indicate more similarity), while dissimilarity measures how different they are (higher values indicate greater difference).

Q: What is Euclidean distance?
A: Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between two points in a multidimensional space, calculated as d(x,y) = sqrt(∑(x_i - y_i)^2).

Q: What is Manhattan distance?
A: Manhattan distance is the sum of absolute differences between coordinates of two points, calculated as d(x,y) = ∑|x_i - y_i|, resembling movement along a grid.

Q: What is Minkowski distance, and how does it generalize other distances?
A: Minkowski distance is a generalized metric d(x,y) = (∑|x_i - y_i|^r)^(1/r). For r=1, it is Manhattan distance; for r=2, it is Euclidean distance; for r→∞, it is the maximum distance.

Q: What is Cosine similarity?
A: Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors, calculated as s(x,y) = (x·y)/(||x||·||y||), efficient for sparse vectors with many zeros.

Q: What is Mahalanobis distance?
A: Mahalanobis distance measures the distance of a point from a dataset’s center, accounting for data covariance, visualized as distances on an ellipse aligned with the data’s main direction.

Q: What properties must a distance measure satisfy to be a metric?
A: A distance measure must satisfy: 1) Non-negativity (d(x,y) ≥ 0), 2) Identity (d(x,y) = 0 ⇔ x = y), 3) Symmetry (d(x,y) = d(y,x)), and 4) Triangle inequality (d(x,y) ≤ d(x,z) + d(z,y)).

Q: What is Hamming distance, and when is it used?
A: Hamming distance measures the number of positions at which two strings of equal length differ, used for nominal data like categorical variables.

Q: How does correlation differ from covariance?
A: Correlation measures the linear relationship between two variables, scaled between -1 and 1, while covariance measures their joint variability, unscaled and affected by variable units.

Q: Why is Cosine similarity invariant to scaling?
A: Cosine similarity is invariant to scaling because it measures the angle between vectors, unaffected by their magnitudes, only their directions.

Q: What is an example of a domain-specific distance measure?
A: In image recognition, a distance measure might focus on shape similarity, while in text analysis, it might prioritize word frequency differences.

Q: What is the significance of the triangle inequality in distance metrics?
A: The triangle inequality ensures that the distance between two points is no greater than the sum of distances through a third point, maintaining geometric consistency.

Q: How is Manhattan distance used in urban planning?
A: Manhattan distance models travel along grid-like street networks, calculating distances as sums of horizontal and vertical movements.

Q: Why is Mahalanobis distance useful for datasets with correlated features?
A: Mahalanobis distance accounts for feature correlations via the covariance matrix, providing a more accurate measure of distance in non-spherical data distributions.

Q: What is the effect of a nonlinear relationship on correlation?
A: Nonlinear relationships result in a correlation of 0, as correlation only measures linear relationships between variables.

## Information-Based Measures
Q: What is mutual information?
A: Mutual information I(X,Y) measures how much information one variable provides about another, calculated as I(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y), where H is entropy. It is zero if variables are independent.

Q: What is KL divergence?
A: Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, or relative entropy, measures the difference between two probability distributions, quantifying information loss when one approximates another.

Q: What is cross entropy?
A: Cross entropy measures the average number of bits needed to encode events from one probability distribution using the optimal code for another, calculated as CE(X,Y) = -∑(P_X(n) * log(P_Y(n))).

Q: How does cross entropy differ from KL divergence?
A: Cross entropy measures total bits to encode events from X using Y’s code, while KL divergence measures extra bits needed, with KL(X||Y) = CE(X,Y) - H(X).

Q: What is an example of mutual information calculation?
A: For student status and grade, mutual information is 0.1624, calculated as I(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) = 0.9928 + 1.4406 - 2.2710.

Q: How is KL divergence used in practice?
A: KL divergence is used to compare probability distributions, such as determining which Gaussian distribution best estimates a given distribution.

Q: What is joint entropy in information theory?
A: Joint entropy H(X,Y) measures the uncertainty of two variables together, calculated as H(X,Y) = -∑∑(P(x,y) * log2(P(x,y))) over all possible x and y.

Q: What is the role of cross entropy in a Bernoulli distribution?
A: In a Bernoulli distribution, cross entropy measures the bits needed to encode outcomes (e.g., 1 with probability p, 0 with 1-p) using an estimated probability q.

Q: What is the Maximal Information Coefficient (MIC)?
A: MIC is an information-based measure that captures both linear and non-linear relationships between variables, though it can be computationally intensive.

Q: Why are information-based measures computationally intensive?
A: Information-based measures like mutual information require calculating probabilities across datasets, involving complex summations or integrations, especially for high-dimensional data.

## Data Representation
Q: What is a basis vector?
A: A basis vector is a vector in a set that is linearly independent and spans a vector space, used to represent data as linear combinations.

Q: What is orthogonality in the context of basis vectors?
A: Orthogonality means basis vectors are perpendicular (their dot product is zero). Orthonormal vectors are orthogonal and have unit length.

Q: What is a basis function?
A: A basis function is a function in a set used to represent other functions as linear combinations, such as Fourier basis functions for signal representation.

Q: What is the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)?
A: The DFT transforms a sequence of data points into a sum of complex exponentials, representing it in the frequency domain, useful for signal analysis.

Q: What is the Discrete-Time Fourier Transform (DTFT)?
A: The DTFT transforms a discrete-time signal into a continuous frequency spectrum, used for analyzing periodic signals.

Q: What is the difference between model-based and data-driven transforms?
A: Model-based transforms (e.g., DFT) use predefined mathematical models, while data-driven transforms learn representations directly from data, often using neural networks.

Q: What is an application of Fourier basis functions?
A: Fourier basis functions are used in signal processing to decompose signals into frequency components for analysis or compression.

Q: What is linear dependency in the context of basis vectors?
A: Linear dependency occurs when one basis vector can be expressed as a linear combination of others, reducing the set’s ability to span the space uniquely.

Q: What is the span of a set of basis vectors?
A: The span is the set of all possible linear combinations of the basis vectors, defining the vector space they cover.

Q: How is the DFT applied in image processing?
A: The DFT is used in image processing to analyze frequency components, enabling tasks like image compression (e.g., JPEG) or filtering.

## Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)
Q: What is a convolutional neural network (CNN)?
A: A CNN is a neural network with convolutional layers that apply filters to input data, extracting features like edges or shapes, commonly used in image processing with minimal preprocessing.

Q: What are the main components of a CNN architecture?
A: A CNN consists of an input layer, hidden layers (convolutional, pooling, fully connected, or normalization), and an output layer for tasks like classification.

Q: What is the role of pooling layers in CNNs?
A: Pooling layers reduce data dimensions by combining neuron cluster outputs (e.g., max or average pooling), decreasing computational load and preventing overfitting.

Q: What is a receptive field in CNNs?
A: A receptive field is the restricted input area a neuron processes in a convolutional layer, typically a small square (e.g., 5x5), influencing feature detection.

Q: What is a deconvolutional neural network?
A: A deconvolutional neural network reverses a CNN, using deconvolutional and unpooling layers to reconstruct or generate data, often used in image generation.

Q: What is the purpose of depthwise separable convolutional layers?
A: Depthwise separable convolutional layers perform spatial convolution per channel followed by pointwise convolution, reducing computational cost and speeding up processing.

Q: What is max pooling, and how does it differ from average pooling?
A: Max pooling selects the maximum value from a neuron cluster, emphasizing strong features, while average pooling computes the average, smoothing features.

Q: Why do CNNs use shared weights in convolutional layers?
A: Shared weights (filters) reduce memory usage and parameters by applying the same filter across receptive fields, enabling feature detection across the input.

Q: What is the Frobenius inner product in CNNs?
A: The Frobenius inner product is used in convolutional layers to compute dot products between input patches and kernels, generating feature maps.

Q: What is the benefit of ReLU activation in CNNs?
A: ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation introduces non-linearity, accelerates convergence, and prevents vanishing gradients, commonly used in convolutional layers.

## Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI)
Q: What is Explainable AI (XAI)?
A: XAI is a field in AI focused on methods that make AI algorithms’ reasoning transparent and comprehensible to humans, addressing the “black box” nature of machine learning.

Q: What are the key principles of XAI?
A: XAI emphasizes transparency (describable processes), interpretability (comprehensible models), and explainability (identifying features contributing to decisions).

Q: What is the difference between interpretability and explainability in XAI?
A: Interpretability is the level of understanding how an AI model works, while explainability is understanding how a specific result was produced.

Q: What are some techniques used for explainability in XAI?
A: Techniques include partial dependency plots, SHAP (Shapley values), feature importance, LIME, multitask learning, and saliency maps.

Q: What is a white-box algorithm in XAI?
A: A white-box algorithm is understandable to experts, with transparent processes and interpretable structures, unlike black-box algorithms, which are hard to explain.

Q: Why is XAI crucial in fields like medicine and finance?
A: XAI ensures trust and understanding in critical applications by justifying decisions, verifying algorithms, and ensuring ethical and legal compliance.

Q: What is the DARPA XAI program?
A: The DARPA XAI program develops “glass box” models that are explainable to humans without sacrificing performance, enabling trust assessment.

Q: What is LIME in the context of XAI?
A: LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) approximates complex model outputs with a simpler interpretable model to explain specific predictions.

Q: What are saliency maps used for in XAI?
A: Saliency maps highlight influential parts of an input (e.g., image regions) that contribute to a model’s output, aiding visual explanation.

Q: How does SHAP contribute to explainability?
A: SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) visualizes feature contributions to predictions using Shapley values, providing consistent and fair explanations.

## Debugging
Q: What is debugging in the context of software engineering?
A: Debugging is the process of identifying the root cause, workarounds, and fixes for bugs in software systems.

Q: What tools are commonly used for debugging software?
A: Tools include debuggers (for monitoring execution), static code analysis tools (e.g., lint), log file analysis, memory dumps, and profiling tools.

Q: Why are high-level languages like Java easier to debug than low-level languages like C?
A: High-level languages offer features like exception handling and type checking, making erratic behavior easier to detect, unlike low-level languages where issues like memory corruption may be silent.

Q: What is the historical origin of the term “bug” in debugging?
A: The term “bug” for a defect dates to 1878, used by Thomas Edison. In computing, a 1940s story about a moth in a Mark II computer relay popularized it.

Q: How does static code analysis aid debugging?
A: Static code analysis tools detect semantic issues (e.g., unassigned variable dereference) in large codebases, complementing compilers and reducing manual review effort.

Q: What are memory dumps, and how are they used in debugging?
A: Memory dumps capture a system’s memory state at a specific time, used to analyze program crashes or memory corruption issues.

Q: What tools are used for debugging hardware or firmware?
A: Tools like oscilloscopes, logic analyzers, and in-circuit emulators perform debugger-like tasks for hardware and low-level software.

Q: What is control flow analysis in debugging?
A: Control flow analysis examines the sequence of program execution to identify logical errors or unexpected paths causing bugs.

## Loss Functions
Q: What is a loss function in machine learning?
A: A loss function maps an event or variable values to a real number representing a “cost,” used to measure the difference between predicted and true values in optimization.

Q: What is the quadratic loss function?
A: The quadratic loss function, or squared error loss, is L(x,t) = C * (t - x)^2, where t is the target and C is a constant, commonly used in least squares optimization.

Q: What is the 0-1 loss function?
A: The 0-1 loss function assigns a loss of 1 if the prediction is incorrect (y_hat ≠ y) and 0 if correct, used in classification to penalize errors.

Q: What is expected loss in statistics?
A: Expected loss is the expected value of a loss function over a data distribution, used in frequentist (risk function) or Bayesian (Bayes risk) decision-making.

Q: What is regret in the context of loss functions?
A: Regret is the difference between the outcome of the best possible decision given known circumstances and the actual decision made, proposed by Leonard J. Savage for non-Bayesian methods.

Q: In what fields are loss functions commonly applied?
A: Loss functions are applied in economics (cost/regret), classification (penalties), actuarial science (benefits vs. premiums), optimal control, and financial risk management.

Q: What is the mean squared error in the context of loss functions?
A: Mean squared error is the risk for a quadratic loss function, calculated as R(theta, theta_hat) = E[(theta - theta_hat)^2], used to estimate the posterior mean.

Q: What is the Huber loss function, and when is it used?
A: Huber loss combines quadratic and linear loss, robust to outliers, used in regression tasks with noisy data or large errors.

Q: How is the 0-1 loss function used in decision theory?
A: The 0-1 loss function penalizes incorrect decisions in classification, guiding decision rules to minimize misclassification errors.

Q: What is the role of loss functions in economic choice under uncertainty?
A: Loss functions, like von Neumann-Morgenstern utility, quantify costs of decisions under uncertainty, maximizing expected utility in economics.

## Transfer Learning
Q: What is transfer learning?
A: Transfer learning is a machine learning technique where knowledge from one task (source domain) is reused to improve performance on a related task (target domain).

Q: How is a domain defined in transfer learning?
A: A domain D consists of a feature space X and a marginal probability distribution P(X), where X is a set of instances.

Q: What are some applications of transfer learning?
A: Applications include text classification, digit recognition, medical imaging, spam filtering, and cross-domain tasks like EMG to EEG signal classification.

Q: What is the historical significance of transfer learning in neural networks?
A: In 1976, Bozinovski and Fulgosi published a paper on transfer learning in neural network training, providing a mathematical and geometrical model.

Q: What is the discriminability-based transfer algorithm?
A: Developed by Lorien Pratt in 1992, it is an early transfer learning algorithm focusing on reusing knowledge based on discriminability between tasks.

Q: How does transfer learning relate to multi-task learning?
A: Transfer learning reuses knowledge from one task to another, while multi-task learning simultaneously learns multiple related tasks, sharing representations.

Q: What is negative transfer in transfer learning?
A: Negative transfer occurs when knowledge from the source task harms performance on the target task, often due to dissimilar domains or tasks.

Q: How did transfer learning impact commercial machine learning success?
A: In 2016, Andrew Ng predicted transfer learning would drive commercial success by enabling efficient model reuse across related tasks.

## Hyperparameter Optimization
Q: What is hyperparameter optimization?
A: Hyperparameter optimization is the process of selecting optimal hyperparameters for a learning algorithm to minimize a loss function, controlling the learning process.

Q: What is grid search in hyperparameter optimization?
A: Grid search exhaustively evaluates a manually specified subset of hyperparameter combinations, using cross-validation or a hold-out set to select the best settings.

Q: How does Bayesian optimization improve hyperparameter tuning?
A: Bayesian optimization builds a probabilistic model of the hyperparameter-to-objective mapping, iteratively evaluating promising configurations to balance exploration and exploitation, requiring fewer evaluations than grid or random search.

Q: What is random search, and how does it compare to grid search?
A: Random search selects hyperparameter combinations randomly, exploring more continuous values than grid search and often outperforming it when few hyperparameters are significant.

Q: What is evolutionary optimization in hyperparameter tuning?
A: Evolutionary optimization uses evolutionary algorithms to search hyperparameter spaces, creating and evolving populations of hyperparameter tuples based on fitness (e.g., cross-validation accuracy).

Q: What is the risk of overfitting in hyperparameter optimization?
A: Optimizing hyperparameters on a validation set risks overfitting to that set, requiring an independent test set or nested cross-validation for unbiased generalization estimates.

Q: What is population-based training in hyperparameter optimization?
A: Population-based training learns hyperparameters and weights simultaneously, replacing poor performers with modified hyperparameters and weights from better performers during training.

Q: What is successive halving in hyperparameter optimization?
A: Successive halving starts with random search, periodically pruning low-performing configurations to focus resources on promising ones, reducing evaluation costs.

Q: What is Hyperband, and how does it improve hyperparameter tuning?
A: Hyperband invokes successive halving multiple times with varying pruning aggressiveness, balancing exploration and exploitation for large search spaces.

Q: How does gradient-based optimization work for hyperparameters?
A: Gradient-based optimization computes gradients of the loss with respect to hyperparameters, using gradient descent to optimize them, suitable for differentiable models like neural networks.

## Transformers
Q: What is a transformer in deep learning?
A: A transformer is a deep learning architecture introduced in 2017, using multi-head attention to process sequences in parallel, replacing recurrent neural networks for tasks like NLP and vision.

Q: What is the role of positional encoding in transformers?
A: Positional encoding provides token position information using sinusoidal functions, enabling transformers to process sequence order without recurrence.

Q: What is scaled dot-product attention in transformers?
A: Scaled dot-product attention computes Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(Q K^T / sqrt(d_k)) V, where Q, K, V are query, key, and value matrices, and scaling stabilizes gradients.

Q: What is the difference between encoder-only and decoder-only transformers?
A: Encoder-only transformers (e.g., BERT) process inputs for representation tasks, while decoder-only transformers (e.g., GPT) use masked self-attention for generation tasks.

Q: What is multi-head attention in transformers?
A: Multi-head attention uses multiple attention heads to process inputs independently, concatenating results to capture different relevance relations, enhancing parallel computation.

Q: What is the role of layer normalization in transformers?
A: Layer normalization stabilizes training by normalizing inputs to sublayers, using pre-LN (before sublayers) or post-LN (after sublayers) to ensure convergence.

Q: What is FlashAttention, and how does it optimize transformers?
A: FlashAttention optimizes attention on GPUs by partitioning computations into cache-fitting blocks, reducing memory usage while maintaining accuracy.

Q: What is speculative decoding in transformers?
A: Speculative decoding accelerates token generation by predicting tokens with a smaller model, verifying with a larger model, and recomputing incorrect predictions to reduce computation time.

Q: What is RoPE in transformer positional encodings?
A: RoPE (Rotary Positional Embedding) encodes token positions as rotations, preserving relative position dot products, improving length extrapolation.

Q: What is ALiBi in transformers?
A: ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases) adds a linear bias matrix to attention scores, enabling transformers to extrapolate to longer sequences.

Q: What is the role of tokenization in transformers?
A: Tokenization converts text into integer tokens using methods like byte pair encoding, mapping them to embeddings for transformer processing.

Q: What is multi-token prediction in transformers?
A: Multi-token prediction predicts multiple future tokens in one pass, trading accuracy for speed by processing embeddings through additional transformer blocks.

## Attention Mechanisms
Q: What is the attention mechanism in machine learning?
A: Attention assigns relative importance to sequence components using soft weights, enabling models to focus on relevant parts, improving context capture in tasks like NLP.

Q: What is self-attention in transformers?
A: Self-attention sets query, key, and value vectors from the same input, allowing a transformer to capture global dependencies within a sequence.

Q: What is masked attention, and where is it used?
A: Masked attention prevents attending to future tokens using a causal mask, used in transformer decoders for autoregressive tasks like text generation.

Q: What is Bahdanau attention?
A: Bahdanau attention, introduced in 2014, uses additive attention with learnable weights, computing Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(tanh(W_Q Q + W_K K)) V.

Q: What is Luong attention?
A: Luong attention, introduced in 2015, uses multiplicative attention with a learnable matrix, computing Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(Q W K^T) V.

Q: How does attention improve over recurrent neural networks?
A: Attention processes all tokens in parallel, directly accessing any sequence part, avoiding RNNs’ sequential processing and vanishing gradient issues.

Q: What is the permutation property of scaled dot-product attention?
A: Scaled dot-product attention is equivariant to query reordering and invariant to key-value reordering, ensuring consistent outputs under permutation.

Q: What is multi-query attention in transformers?
A: Multi-query attention shares key/value matrices across heads, reducing memory and boosting inference speed in transformer models.

## Vision Transformers
Q: What is a vision transformer (ViT)?
A: A vision transformer is a transformer adapted for computer vision, splitting images into patches, mapping them to vectors, and processing them with a transformer encoder.

Q: How does a vision transformer differ from a convolutional neural network?
A: ViTs use attention to capture global dependencies across image patches, while CNNs use convolutional filters for local feature extraction, with ViTs being less data-efficient but higher-capacity.

Q: What is an application of vision transformers?
A: Vision transformers are applied in image recognition, segmentation, weather prediction, and autonomous driving, leveraging their ability to model long-range dependencies.

Q: What is the Swin Transformer?
A: The Swin Transformer, introduced in 2021, uses sliding window attention and pyramid processing to improve efficiency and accuracy in vision tasks, excelling on datasets like COCO.

Q: What is a masked autoencoder in the context of vision transformers?
A: A masked autoencoder extends ViTs to unsupervised learning by masking image patches and reconstructing them, improving feature learning.

Q: How does the ViT process image patches?
A: ViT splits an image into fixed-size patches, linearly maps each to a vector (patch embedding), adds positional encodings, and processes them with transformer encoders.

## Sequential Decision Making
Q: What is sequential decision making?
A: Sequential decision making involves making a series of decisions over time to optimize an objective, like maximizing rewards, considering states, actions, and probabilistic transitions.

Q: What is a Markov decision process (MDP)?
A: An MDP is a model for sequential decision making under uncertainty, defined as a 4-tuple (S, A, P_a, R_a), with states, actions, transition probabilities, and rewards.

Q: What is the optimization objective in an MDP?
A: The objective is to find a policy pi that maximizes the expected discounted cumulative reward, typically E[∑(gamma^t * R_a_t(s_t, s_(t+1)))], where gamma is the discount factor.

Q: What is the Markov property in MDPs?
A: The Markov property states that the next state and reward depend only on the current state and action, not on prior history.

Q: What is a policy in the context of MDPs?
A: A policy pi is a function mapping states to actions, determining the action pi(s) to take in state s, potentially probabilistic.

Q: What is the difference between episodic and generative simulators in MDPs?
A: Episodic simulators start from an initial state, producing state-action-reward trajectories, while generative simulators sample next states and rewards for any state-action pair.

Q: What is the H-step return in MDPs?
A: The H-step return maximizes the expected sum of rewards over the first H steps, E[∑(R_a_t(s_t, s_(t+1)))], with equal weights, used in learning theory.

Q: How does the discount factor affect MDP optimization?
A: A lower discount factor (gamma) prioritizes early rewards, while a higher gamma (near 1) emphasizes long-term rewards, affecting policy decisions.

## Q-Learning
Q: What is Q-learning?
A: Q-learning is a model-free reinforcement learning algorithm that learns an optimal policy by assigning values (Q-values) to state-action pairs, maximizing expected rewards.

Q: How does the Q-learning algorithm update Q-values?
A: Q-values are updated using the Bellman equation: Q_new(s,a) = (1 - alpha) * Q(s,a) + alpha * (R + gamma * max_a Q(s',a)), where alpha is the learning rate and gamma is the discount factor.

Q: What is the role of the discount factor in Q-learning?
A: The discount factor (gamma) weights future rewards, with gamma = 0 focusing on immediate rewards and gamma near 1 prioritizing long-term rewards.

Q: What is the learning rate in Q-learning, and how does it affect learning?
A: The learning rate (alpha) determines how much new information overrides old Q-values. Alpha = 0 relies on prior knowledge; alpha = 1 uses only recent data.

Q: What is deep Q-learning, and how does it differ from standard Q-learning?
A: Deep Q-learning uses convolutional neural networks to approximate Q-values for large state spaces, addressing instability with experience replay to reduce correlations.

Q: What is double Q-learning, and why is it used?
A: Double Q-learning uses two value functions (Q^A, Q^B) to counter overestimation in noisy environments, updating one for action selection and the other for evaluation.

Q: What are the limitations of standard Q-learning?
A: Standard Q-learning is limited to discrete spaces, requiring discretization for continuous spaces, which can be inefficient due to the curse of dimensionality.

Q: What is experience replay in deep Q-learning?
A: Experience replay stores past state-action-reward transitions and samples them randomly to reduce correlations and stabilize training in deep Q-learning.

Q: What is the role of initial Q-values in Q-learning?
A: High initial Q-values encourage exploration, as updates lower untried actions’ values, while rewards can reset conditions for immediate learning.

## Autoencoders
Q: What is an autoencoder?
A: An autoencoder is a neural network for unsupervised learning, encoding input data into a lower-dimensional representation and decoding it to reconstruct the input.

Q: What is the purpose of a variational autoencoder (VAE)?
A: A VAE models data as a distribution using variational Bayesian methods, suitable for generative tasks by sampling from a latent space.

Q: What is a denoising autoencoder (DAE)?
A: A DAE trains with corrupted inputs (e.g., added noise) to reconstruct clean inputs, enhancing robustness by optimizing E[d(x, D(E(T(x))))].

Q: What are the advantages of deep autoencoders over shallow ones?
A: Deep autoencoders reduce computational cost, require less training data, and improve compression through multiple layers compared to shallow autoencoders.

Q: What is a sparse autoencoder (SAE)?
A: A sparse autoencoder enforces sparse codes with few active hidden units, using techniques like k-sparse functions or sparsity regularization to enhance feature selection.

Q: What is a contractive autoencoder (CAE)?
A: A contractive autoencoder adds a regularization loss to minimize the encoder’s Jacobian Frobenius norm, ensuring small input changes yield small code changes.

Q: What is the minimum description length autoencoder (MDL-AE)?
A: An MDL-AE minimizes the total description length (code length + reconstruction error) per the Minimum Description Length principle, ensuring compact representations.

Q: What is an example application of autoencoders?
A: Autoencoders are used in anomaly detection, identifying rare instances by reconstructing inputs and flagging those with high reconstruction errors.

Q: What is a concrete autoencoder (CAE)?
A: A concrete autoencoder selects a fixed number of features in the latent space using a continuous relaxation of the categorical distribution, enabling backpropagation.

Q: How are deep belief networks used with autoencoders?
A: Deep belief networks pretrain deep autoencoders by treating layer pairs as restricted Boltzmann machines, followed by backpropagation fine-tuning.

## Anomaly Detection
Q: What is anomaly detection?
A: Anomaly detection identifies rare items, events, or observations that deviate significantly from most data, indicating a different generating mechanism or inconsistency.

Q: What are the three categories of anomaly detection?
A: Categories are: 1) Supervised (labeled normal/abnormal data), 2) Semi-supervised (some labeled data, modeling normal behavior), and 3) Unsupervised (unlabeled data, detecting anomalies).

Q: Why is supervised anomaly detection rare?
A: Supervised anomaly detection is rare due to limited labeled data and class imbalance, making it challenging to train classifiers effectively.

Q: What is an example of a specific anomaly detection definition?
A: In a univariate Gaussian, a point is an outlier if its z-score exceeds a threshold, indicating significant deviation from the mean.

Q: How does semi-supervised anomaly detection work?
A: Semi-supervised anomaly detection models normal behavior with labeled data, testing new instances’ likelihood against this model to identify anomalies.

Q: What is the role of unsupervised anomaly detection in cybersecurity?
A: Unsupervised anomaly detection identifies unusual patterns (e.g., network intrusions) in unlabeled data, flagging potential threats without prior labeling.

Q: How does anomaly detection differ from noise?
A: Anomalies indicate significant deviations from normal behavior, often of interest, while noise is random variation that does not reflect a different mechanism.

Q: What is an application of anomaly detection in medicine?
A: Anomaly detection identifies rare medical conditions or abnormal test results by detecting deviations from typical patient data patterns.
